## TL;DR

Robot policies inherit a demonstrator’s hesitation and inconsistent timing. A learned path-dependent speed distribution retimes demonstrations, while a constrained controller executes the predicted tempo.

## Problem

Imitation learning copies when a teleoperator moves as well as what they do. The same useful path can therefore come with inconsistent action timing, pauses and hesitation. Existing acceleration methods often need task-specific fits, labels or prescribed rates, while uniformly shortening every step ignores where precision matters. This paper learns a speed distribution shared across tasks and transfers the pace of faster demonstrators to all source paths.

## Key Idea

**Separate the geometric path from its clock.** Preserve the demonstration’s motion path and infer how long each local interval should take.

- A speed prior reads an instruction, a path window, wrist images and a source/target domain label, then predicts a distribution of local speeds.
- A chosen quantile yields a clock: the median gives consistent training targets, while higher quantiles supply faster tempo channels.
- A constrained controller follows the requested pace where feasible and slows locally when robot limits bind.

### A speed distribution over a path

Path progress combines translation, rotation and gripper opening in one arc-length metric. Paths are sampled every 2 cm and processed in 17-node windows. A transformer predicts categorical arm and gripper log-speed distributions over 256 bins from 1 to 5,000 mm/s. A two-hot cross-entropy objective models the effective speed determined by the slower actuator rather than regressing one average speed.

The first-stage prior uses DROID teleoperation and FastUMI-100K handheld demonstrations. To transfer pace without confusing visual domain with timing, training drops images with probability 0.5. At retiming, a classifier-free-guidance-style difference between target and source logits without images adjusts the source prediction with images. When grippers differ, gripper speed retains its source label.

Integrating inverse quantile speed produces a replacement clock. π0.5 is fine-tuned on joint-action chunks under the median clock, with additional dimensions predicting q90, q95 and q99 tempo. These channels specify time per action; they do not simply multiply all movements by one constant. For the three real-robot fine-tuning tasks, the prior is adapted per task using the shortest quarter of its handheld episodes as the target domain, then retimes all demonstrations. Thus the shared first-stage prior does not eliminate task adaptation in this experiment.

### Executing variable tempo

**Draft** fits a minimum-jerk path through tolerance boxes and times it under velocity, acceleration and torque limits. **Splice** joins it to the running plan at the earliest feasible point reached on schedule. **Smooth** solves a final minimum-jerk program while preserving position, velocity and acceleration continuity. Tempo is an upper bound on execution speed, not a promise that every requested speed is physically achievable.

## Main Results

- **RoboLab, 120 tasks × 10 episodes:** Table 1 reports 60.8% success at 2.38× realized speed for q99, versus 53.0% at 2.48× for uniformly accelerated execution. Integrated squared jerk falls from 2.41 to 1.16, about 52%. These operating points have similar, rather than identical, measured speeds; motion-quality metrics use episodes completed by every compared execution. Simulation uses joint-target interpolation without the proposed controller, isolating the clocks.
- **Real cup stacking, same proposed controller:** the recorded-timing baseline takes 6.02 s and the retimed q95 policy 3.50 s, a 1.72× speed-up, both succeeding in 29/30 trials. Times average successful rollouts. Training only on the fastest quarter yields 9/30 successes.
- **Controller effect, isolated from retiming:** the recorded-timing towel policy succeeds in 4/30 trials with PD and 29/30 with the proposed controller. This benefit belongs to execution control and should be separated from the learned-clock contribution.

## Achievements and Limitations

### Achievements

- Learns a reusable, task-dependent pace distribution and transfers it to paths not demonstrated by the faster target group.
- Keeps the diversity of all demonstrations rather than discarding slower episodes, which performs better than fast-only behavior cloning in the reported experiments.
- Connects variable-tempo VLA chunks to continuous constrained execution; simulation and real-robot comparisons distinguish the timing and controller effects.

### Limitations

- **Author-stated:** the prior transfers speeds someone has demonstrated; it cannot accelerate beyond the observed pace support.
- **Author-stated deployment boundary:** experiments are in a controlled laboratory. Deployment near humans requires additional safety validation.
- **🤖 AI analysis:** the complete real-world stack is tested on one robot platform and five tasks. Its robustness across robot dynamics and richer contact tasks remains unestablished.

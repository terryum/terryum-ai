AI agents are rapidly taking over the functional work people used to do. Finding information, writing code, producing documents, and comparing options are already faster and cheaper with AI. So how should a person prove their worth? It clearly will not be by competing with AI on the same plane of functional roles.

In [The Talent Standard in the Age of AI Agents](/en/posts/260528-hiring-for-agentic-execution), I wrote that people need entrepreneurial ownership. If AI produces functional outputs, people must direct multiple agents and turn those outputs into business outcomes. They must decide which problem to solve, verify the result, and close the loop all the way to final metrics such as revenue, customers, and cost. The more agents a person directs, the heavier their judgment and responsibility become. That is why future organizations may need a small number of exceptional people more than a large number of average ones.

Those few do not exist alone. As I argued in [Value Comes From Finite Resources](/en/posts/260618-value-from-finite-resources), the more AI lowers the price of making things, the more the final bottleneck remains with people. Inside a team, colleagues must be able to entrust work to one another. Outside the team, customers must be able to trust the promises made by a person and a company. Even if one person combines their own time with AI time to produce enormous output, it will not become durable value without relationships that can receive that output and turn it into the next undertaking. Organizations in the AI era will depend less on broad, shallow labor pools and more on narrow, deep, high-trust networks in which exceptional individuals trust one another.

In the past, education and company names stood in for this trust. Admission to a good school was indirect proof that someone had passed a certain threshold of intelligence and diligence. A high salary at a large company signaled that their ability had been recognized. So the strategy of starting at a prestigious company, moving to a larger one, and raising one's salary step by step worked fairly well. Those signals have not disappeared entirely. But the distance between the school and company names listed on a résumé and the person's actual output keeps widening.

The larger the organization, the farther an individual's work can be from the final outcome. Someone may produce the required documents, coordinate meetings, and execute established procedures well, yet still find it difficult to know what changed for customers or revenue. Of course, the best outcome would be to take real responsibility at a strong company and produce real business results. But such opportunities are rare for new graduates. Sometimes, rather than becoming a small component in a famous company, working in a small organization—meeting customers, improving the product, and seeing one's judgment affect revenue and survival—builds capability for the AI era much faster.

The important questions are no longer only “Which company did you join?” They are “Who did you work with?” and “What reputation did you leave inside which high-trust network?” That matters more than becoming an anonymous employee at a large company. By “who,” I mean a leader who stands on an important branch of the industry in which I want to build my career: someone I can trust and follow, someone I would want as a long-term industry colleague and mentor even after we no longer work at the same company. Being entrusted with an important problem by such a leader, exceeding expectations, and earning a reputation through that work creates a stronger career than a company name on one line of a résumé.

That is how the high-trust network I described in [Why Work at an Early Startup?](/en/posts/260523-why-work-at-early-startups) is built. Only by passing through pressure together can you learn who steps forward in a crisis, who takes responsibility when the work grows beyond the original promise, and who finishes what matters and leaves something useful behind when they depart. Trust takes time. Looking diligent for a few days is not enough, and neither is kindness when everything is going well. You enter a higher tier of trust only after finishing hard work together and leaving others with the desire to entrust the next challenge to you again.

That is why it saddens me to see people at the beginning of their careers moving quickly in pursuit of a slightly better company name or a slightly higher salary. I understand why they do it. But when you move too often, no one gets enough time to see what you can truly prove. You also lose the chance to climb the ladder of a high-trust network. What remains is only a list of schools and companies, and you may end up repeating prescribed roles in organizations that value those names. You become just another anonymous employee of a large company.

If you have met good people and been entrusted with an important problem, it may be worth proving your value beyond your salary at least once. Produce results until your compensation looks small compared with your contribution, then discuss compensation. If the leader you trusted and followed is reasonable, they will raise your salary and responsibility—or, if the organization cannot reward you at that level, help and encourage you to move to a better opportunity. The first path leads to greater responsibility, and the second to broader opportunity. Either way, mutual trust remains.

Please do not build relationships in the industry where you hope to grow your career as though you were quitting a convenience-store job after a few days with a one-line message. As AI agents take over more functional work, what remains for people is internal trust among colleagues, external trust with customers, and the individual's ability to produce results on top of that trust. The opportunity I receive, and the trust that sent that opportunity my way, becomes the foothold for climbing to the next level of trust. A ladder of growth does not appear automatically whenever you move sideways to a larger company or a higher salary.

I hope junior professionals beginning new chapters will pass through greater opportunities and more chances to prove themselves alongside exceptional people—and rise as exceptional individuals among them.

In my previous essay, [The First Condition for Human Workers](/en/posts/260810-human-workers-first-condition-trust), I argued that enduring trust is the most valuable resource a talented person can build. And in [Talent in the AI Era](/en/posts/260528-hiring-for-agentic-execution), I suggested four questions for assessing someone you are meeting for the first time.

- Intelligence: "Are they smart?"
- Execution: "Do they get things done?"
- Character: "Do I want to spend a lot of time around them?"
- Entrepreneurial ownership: "Can they turn ambiguity into business outcomes?"

We also need to ask the question from the other side: to grow into the kind of person the AI era needs, what kind of company should I work for? I think the following four questions help us judge a good workplace.

- Frontier ways of working: "Can I work at the frontier, in the way the frontier works?"
- Proximity to problems: "Am I close enough to a real problem to redefine it?"
- Asymmetric assets: "Does this company have something AI cannot easily commoditize?"
- Organizational honesty: "Does the reality inside match the story outside?"

The first question is whether I can work in the way the frontier works. In the AI era, how you work somewhere matters as much as which company you work for. You should be able to try new AI agents quickly, divide work among multiple agents to experiment with new forms of automation, and keep changing your workflow by freely using open source and outside knowledge. The point is more than access to a few recent tools. Can the company change roles, organizational structures, and decision-making as technology changes? When my own way of working can stand at the frontier of work in the AI era, both the company and I can ride the AI wave together.

The second question is how close I am to the problem. In the AI era, methods become cheaper while customers' problems become more valuable. I can create meaningful innovation for customers when I see their problems firsthand, redefine the problem itself, and connect it with frontier ways of working. A good company therefore offers an environment where I can work close to real problems and combine AI, robots, people, and equipment into solutions, rather than simply implement requirements someone else has written. A good company does more than pass problems on to employees; it lets employees get close to the problems themselves.

The third question is whether the company has asymmetric assets that AI cannot easily commoditize. The price of general knowledge, software development, market research, and content creation will keep falling. Actual customer relationships, deep industry knowledge, proprietary data, factories and equipment, supply chains, brand trust, regulatory experience, and tacit knowledge from the field are much harder to replicate. Living in the AI era does not necessarily mean you should join an AI startup. Adding the new leverage of AI to the tangible assets of an established industry may create opportunities that other companies cannot easily match.

The fourth question is organizational honesty. Just as character matters in a person, a company has a kind of character too. In fields such as AI and robotics, where expectations and hype run high, the gap between the vision presented to the world and the actual state of the technology must not become too wide. A good company can talk about an ambitious future while being honest about where it stands today. It should call a demo a demo, acknowledge a failure as a failure, and use disappointing results as lessons for the next step instead of hiding them. I often see people leave because they joined expecting one thing and found something entirely different. A large gap between expectations and reality can be deeply disillusioning, so I hope you choose a company that is honest about itself.

The distinctions between startups and large companies, or AI companies and traditional industries, matter less than we might think. Even an AI company is unlikely to be a good workplace if it is far from real problems, builds things anyone can replicate, and has little beyond impressive PR. Conversely, an established manufacturer can be an excellent workplace if it lets people experiment freely with new technology, work directly on real customer and operational problems, amplify decades of accumulated assets with AI, and make substantive progress one step at a time. Real growth comes from solving real problems.

I want to help build that kind of workplace myself. I spent six years as a startup founder, trying to create the freest startup culture I could. I am now in my second year as an executive responsible for Physical AI at COSMAX, a traditional manufacturing company, yet the workplace I want to build remains much the same: one where integrity and responsibly seeing work through build trust; where that trust lets people work with greater freedom and ease, with consideration for one another; and where people who want to make something happen have the foundations, room, and support to do so, with obstacles cleared from their path.

Having moved into an environment with more resources than a startup, I find it harder in some ways to hire people with genuine commitment. I hope to work with [people suited to the AI era](/en/posts/260528-hiring-for-agentic-execution)—people so smart, decent, capable of executing and finishing, and full of entrepreneurial ownership that I think, "I would love to leave and start a company with this person."
